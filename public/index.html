An open letter to the IAU

Pluto is a planet.

It's round. It orbits the Sun. It has five moons, mountains of ice and a big heart-shaped glacier. What more do you want?

What happened

Pluto didn't change. The rules did.

Nobody discovered anything wrong with Pluto. What happened is that astronomers started finding other icy worlds out past Neptune, and in 2005 one of them, Eris, looked as if it might be bigger than Pluto. Suddenly there was a real chance the solar system would end up with fifteen planets, or fifty.

So on 24 August 2006, on the last day of a conference in Prague, the International Astronomical Union voted on a new definition of "planet". Of the thousands of astronomers who had attended, 424 were still there to raise their cards. Pluto was out by teatime.

Under the new definition, a planet has to pass three tests:

✓

It orbits the Sun

Every 248 years, like clockwork.

✓

It's massive enough to pull itself round

Pluto is a sphere 2,377 km across. It's actually wider than Eris, the object that started the whole mess.

✗

It has "cleared the neighbourhood" around its orbit

This is the one Pluto fails, because it shares its part of space with the rest of the Kuiper Belt. It's also the one that doesn't make much sense. Keep reading.

The problem with rule three

A planet shouldn't depend on its postcode.

The first two rules describe the object itself. The third describes where it lives. That makes it a strange way to classify things, and it falls apart quickly when you push on it.

01

The Earth test

Move Earth out to where Pluto is and the orbit is so long and so crowded that Earth wouldn't clear it either. Same planet, different address, and it stops being a planet.

02

Neptune fails too

Pluto's orbit crosses Neptune's. If Pluto hasn't cleared its neighbourhood because Neptune is in it, then Neptune hasn't cleared its own because Pluto is in it. Nobody seems keen to demote Neptune.

03

Nobody else works this way

A star is a star whether it's alone or in a cluster. A mountain doesn't stop being a mountain because it's in a range. Only planets get judged on their neighbours.

Define a planet by what it is, not by where it happens to be.
Then we actually went there

And it was spectacular.

NASA's New Horizons left Earth in January 2006, while Pluto was still officially a planet. By the time it flew past on 14 July 2015 it had been demoted, which seems rude. What it found was anything but a dull lump of ice.

🤍

A heart

A bright plain of frozen nitrogen about a thousand kilometres wide, now called Tombaugh Regio. It's young, smooth and slowly churning.

🏔️

Real mountains

Peaks of water ice several kilometres high. At those temperatures, ice is as hard as rock.

🌫️

A blue sky

A thin atmosphere with layers of haze that scatter sunlight blue, a bit like ours.

🌊

Maybe an ocean

There are good reasons to think there's liquid water hidden under the ice.

🌑

Five moons

Charon, Nix, Hydra, Kerberos and Styx. Charon is half Pluto's width, so the two orbit each other like a double act.

🕊️

Its discoverer

The probe carried some of Clyde Tombaugh's ashes, so he got to visit the planet he found.

Pluto has friends

We are not alone.

2006

The American Dialect Society picks plutoed, meaning "demoted or devalued", as its word of the year.

2007

New Mexico, where Tombaugh lived and worked, declares that Pluto is a planet whenever it passes over the state's night sky.

2009

The Illinois Senate, in Tombaugh's home state, passes a resolution saying Pluto was unfairly downgraded.

2015

New Horizons sends back the first close-up photos. Half the internet falls in love with a heart-shaped glacier.

Today

The debate still hasn't gone away, and plenty of planetary scientists still call Pluto a planet anyway.

Stand up and be counted

Bring Pluto back.

It won't change anything at the IAU. But Pluto is 5.9 billion kilometres away and could use the moral support.
